What this studio asks of you

Each group is to select one topic, and each topic should have only 2 groups working on it. The two groups must not investigate the same area, so talk to each other and plan your work better. Your research will produce a sharp question, real interviews, personas backed by evidence, and a journey map that points to where design should intervene.

By the end you will have practised four things:

  • Framing: turning an ambiguous statement into a researchable question.
  • Fieldwork: recruiting participants and running structured interviews, plus a survey to test what you heard.
  • Synthesis: turning qualitative data into personas and insights, not a wall of sticky notes.
  • Journey mapping: finding the moments that matter and proposing an intervention for each.

Two groups work on each brief. Both start from the same statement but must choose different user segments, so we can compare findings in the debrief.

How to read a brief

Every brief follows the same pattern: everyone did the sensible thing, the outcome still fails, and two roles see it differently.

  • Statement: what you are given. It is loose on purpose.
  • Hidden tension: the contradiction we suspect. Treat it as a hypothesis, not a fact.
  • People to talk to: the roles you must interview, and compare, to test the tension.
  • Sharper questions: starting points for your interview guide. Rewrite them; do not copy them.
  • Watch out for: the traps earlier teams have fallen into.

The five briefs

Brief 1: Planned, Bought, Still Wasted

PlannerCook

Many households shop with a plan or a list, and still end up throwing food away or ordering something else for dinner. Understand what happens between the shopping bag and the plate.
Hidden tension
The person who plans, the person who shops, the person who cooks and the people who eat are often different, or the same person at different energy levels. A plan made at 11am rarely survives 7pm.
People to talk to
The household's planner or buyer and its cook, or the cook and those who eat. Compare their accounts of the same week.
Sharper questions
At what moment does the plan get abandoned, and what replaces it? Who decides that food is no longer good?
Watch out for
Moralising about waste, and "build a meal-planning app" solutions. Describe behaviour; do not judge it.

Brief 2: Handed Over, Still Independent

Older adultHelper

Many older adults managed their own money, paperwork and travel for decades. Now, for banking, pensions, tickets and government services, they hand over the phone or the task to someone else. Both sides often say it works fine. Explore what is handed over, and what isn't.
Hidden tension
Help can work and still cost something: independence, understanding of what was done, or safety when PINs and OTPs are shared. The helper carries a burden the older adult may not see.
People to talk to
An older adult (60+) and their helper (family member, neighbour or shopkeeper), interviewed separately, then compared.
Sharper questions
What do older adults still do alone, and why those tasks? What does the helper never say out loud? What does the older adult not know about what was done on their behalf?
Watch out for
Treating older adults as one "low-tech" group. Never ask for account details, PINs or credentials, and handle fraud stories with care. Since you may be the helper in your own family, note your own bias.

Brief 3: Jobs Everywhere, Candidates Nowhere

StudentScreener

Final-year students and recent graduates often say there are not enough good opportunities. Recruiters and placement officers often say they cannot find the right candidates. Both are describing the same hiring season. Understand what each side actually experiences.
Hidden tension
Each side sees only its own half of the process. Students cannot see how they are screened, and recruiters cannot see how students decide where to apply.
People to talk to
Students (top, average and recent graduates) and someone who screens or places them (recruiter, placement officer, HR, or a senior who recently interviewed candidates).
Sharper questions
What signals does each side believe it is sending, and what does the other side actually receive? Where do students get information, and whom do they trust?
Watch out for
"Build a job portal", interviewing only friends, and steering participants into talking about self-worth after rejection. Keep questions on process.

Brief 4: Neatly Lost

StorerSearcher

Most households believe they are reasonably organised. Yet when something is urgently needed, whether a document, a tool, a charger or a piece of clothing, it can't be found, or only one person knows where it is. Understand what really happens.
Hidden tension
The person who stored the item and the person who searches for it have different mental maps of the house.
People to talk to
One storer and one searcher from the same household, interviewed separately, then compared.
Scope
Each group picks one category: important documents, tools and hardware, gadgets and cables, seasonal items, or children's school and hobby items. Food is excluded to avoid overlap with Brief 1.
Sharper questions
How do people decide where something goes? When is a duplicate bought instead of searching? Who is the household's keeper of knowledge, and what happens when they are away?
Watch out for
"Build an inventory app with QR codes". Ask why earlier attempts (labels, boxes, lists) failed in your participants' homes. Never comment on tidiness. Prefer high-stakes cases, such as a passport or property papers needed the same day.

Brief 5: Agreed, Explained, Still Disputed

TenantLandlord

Tenants in hostels, PGs and rented flats often say they were not told important things before moving in. Landlords, owners and wardens often say the rules were clear. Understand what was actually communicated, and when.
Hidden tension
Both sides may be telling the truth. Rules can be stated without being understood, and expectations can exist without being spoken.
People to talk to
Tenants in different arrangements (hostel, PG, shared flat) and landlords, PG owners or wardens.
Sharper questions
What did each side believe was agreed, and when did they first find out it wasn't? What happens when something goes wrong: repairs, rent, deposits, guests?
Watch out for
"Build a rental app", and hearing only the tenant's version.

Ground rules

Four rules apply to every group. They are what make your findings defensible.

  1. Write your hypothesis first, then try to disprove it. Before fieldwork, state the hidden tension as one testable sentence. During interviews, look for evidence against it. If the tension turns out not to be real, that is a valid finding and you report it.
  2. Sample beyond your friends. At least half of your participants must come from outside your own branch, year or friend circle. Your group must include at least one person from every role listed under People to talk to for your brief.
  3. Compare within pairs. Where your brief names two roles, interview them separately and set their accounts side by side. The contradictions are the raw material for personas and journey maps.
  4. If possible, sort out ethics and consent before fieldwork.
    • Give every participant a short consent script: who you are, why you are asking, what you will record, and that they can stop at any time.
    • Ask about process and behaviour, never about identity, finances, credentials or anything you would not want asked of your own family.
    • Do not record audio, video or photos without explicit permission. Home walkthroughs (Briefs 1 and 4) need consent from every adult present.
    • Have your interview guide reviewed by the instructor before your first interview.

What you hand in

Each deliverable has a minimum evidence bar. Falling below it costs marks in the rubric, however polished the slides are.

DeliverableWhat it must containMinimum evidence bar
Research question and hypothesisYour reframed question, the hidden tension as a testable sentence, and who you will talk to and whyWritten before fieldwork; approved by the instructor
Interview guideOpen questions grouped by journey stage, a consent script, and a note on how you will avoid leading questionsReviewed by the instructor before your first interview
InterviewsNotes or transcripts, tagged by participant code and roleAt least 6 interviews, covering every role named in your brief
SurveyShort survey used to test what interviews suggested, not to replace themAt least 30 responses; each question traceable to an interview finding
Personas2 or 3 personas built from patterns in your dataEvery persona cites specific quotes and behaviours from named participant codes
InsightsThe headline findingsEach written as "because... so... which means..." and linked to evidence; a theme label alone does not count
Journey mapOne end-to-end map for one persona or role pair, with emotions and touchpoints2 or 3 moments that matter, each justified with evidence
InterventionsOne intervention per moment that mattersEach with a way to test it and a note on what would prove it wrong
ReflectionWhat surprised you, what your hypothesis got wrong, what you would do differentlyOne page maximum

What a good insight reads like"Because the person who stored the passport is not the person who needs it, and only one of them knows the storage logic, the household buys duplicates instead of searching, which means any solution has to transfer the storer's reasoning, not just the item's location."

How you will be graded

Weights below are a suggested starting point out of 100; the instructor may adjust them. Synthesis and journey mapping carry the most marks because they are where research turns into design direction.

CriterionWeightStrong workWeak work
Framing15The question is specific and researchable; the hypothesis can be proved wrong; the chosen segment is justifiedThe question restates the brief, or is really a solution ("build an app for...")
Fieldwork20Interview guide avoids leading questions; participants cover all named roles; the survey tests interview findingsOnly friends interviewed; questions ask for opinions rather than past behaviour; survey unrelated to interviews
Synthesis25Personas rest on patterns and cite participant codes; insights follow "because... so... which means..."; contradictions between roles are analysedPersonas are invented demographics; insights are theme labels; only one side heard
Journey map and intervention25Moments that matter are chosen with evidence; each intervention is testable and links back to a specific insightMap is a generic process diagram; interventions are feature lists with no evidence
Ethics and sampling10Consent script used; sampling rule met; participant sheet kept; no sensitive data collectedMissing consent; participants overlap with another group; credentials or private details requested
Reflection and communication5Honest account of what the hypothesis got wrong; clear, concise presentationOnly reports what went as expected

Where teams most often lose marks: hearing only one side of the story, presenting themes instead of insights, and jumping to a solution before the evidence supports one.
